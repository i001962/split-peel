# Final Whistle Show Bible

This bible defines the creative engine for Final Whistle with Split & Peel. Use it before brainstorming episode ideas, drafting scripts, adding characters, or deciding whether a joke belongs in the show.

## North Star

Final Whistle is a football cartoon about two lovable, obsessive hosts who treat the beautiful game like it is tactics, romance, civic religion, neighborhood gossip, and live theater all at once.

The show can do impossible cartoon things, but it must stay emotionally honest about football and fans. The game matters. The supporters matter. The characters may be ridiculous, but their love is real.

## Core Promise

- Fast football commentary with actual match texture.
- Cartoon escalation that visualizes how fans feel inside.
- Jokes rooted in character flaws, not random noise.
- Respect for the game, even when roasting the discourse around it.
- Recurring bits that reward regular viewers without blocking new ones.

## Pillars

### 1. Honor The Beautiful Game

Use real football specificity: teams, tactics, player roles, managers, fixtures, key moments, fan rituals, transfer logic, rivalries, chants, heartbreak, and joy.

The show may exaggerate football culture, but it should not treat football as fake, simple, or stupid. The joke is usually the human response around the game, not the game itself.

### 2. Cartoon The Feeling

When a fan says a referee ruined their day, the cartoon can make the referee's whistle crack the moon. When Peel distrusts xG, the xG chart can become a haunted weather map. When Split catches a tactical pattern, stadium floodlights can rearrange into a conspiracy board.

The impossible image should express a true football emotion.

### 3. Character First

Every joke should sound like it could only come from Split, Peel, or another clearly defined character. If any host could say the line, rewrite it through a character flaw, belief, obsession, or relationship.

### 4. Lovable Before Savage

The show can roast bad takes, meltdowns, pundit nonsense, superstition, and overconfidence. It should avoid cruelty toward ordinary fans, vulnerable people, players' bodies, identity, tragedy, or real harassment targets.

The hosts can be sharp, but the show should feel like it is laughing from inside the football family.

### 5. Escalate With Logic

Good cartoon escalation is not random. It follows a simple emotional chain:

```text
small football problem -> character misreads it -> worse solution -> public consequence -> impossible visual payoff -> emotional button
```

## Show Format

Default format: Split on the left, Peel on the right, stadium gantry booth energy, pitch and stands behind them, football graphics in the center or above the desk.

Episode types:

- `game-week-preview`: cover the full slate. Build comedy from too many fixtures, early narratives, doomed predictions, and emotional overinvestment before kickoff.
- `match-event`: focus on one selected match. Use live status, key moments, score state, and fan chatter.
- `recap`: lead with the result, then turn key moments into argument, confession, ritual, or cartoon trial.
- `general`: use trends, transfers, injuries, manager pressure, fan culture, or recurring club sagas.
- `outtake`: behind-the-scenes hot-mic energy. Messier, sharper, less polished, but still character-driven and not hateful.

## Character Engines

### Split

Split is the lead host. She is fast, precise, tactical, mischievous, and emotionally fluent about football. She sees patterns before everyone else and has a dangerous talent for making correct points sound like traps.

Core wants:

- Keep the show moving.
- Make the analysis smarter than the room expects.
- Protect the love of the game from lazy takes.
- Let Peel be ridiculous without letting him drive off a cliff.

Comic flaws:

- Over-patterns everything.
- Can weaponize silence.
- Cannot resist a handsome player with tactical utility.
- Gets too precise when emotionally rattled.

Usual joke shapes:

- Dry correction after Peel says something accidentally wild.
- Hyper-specific tactical diagnosis of an absurd situation.
- Calmly naming the exact moment a fanbase lost its mind.
- Letting a pause do damage.

Cartoon possibilities:

- Her tactics board becomes a living crime map.
- Her side-eye freezes a replay frame.
- She diagrams a player's run with surgical lasers.
- She turns fan chaos into a clean three-point framework, then loses the thread over a player being hot.

### Peel

Peel is the older senior-statesman co-host. He is warm, theatrical, romantic, old-school, and overconfident in the eye test. He respects Split, but often wanders into chaos before realizing she has already built a fence.

Core wants:

- Defend football feeling against sterile analysis.
- Tell the biggest version of the story.
- Be right through vibes.
- Make every fan comment feel like a pub debate at full volume.

Comic flaws:

- Thinks looking like a baller is evidence.
- Distrusts charts that look like homework.
- Accidentally creates double meanings.
- Escalates normal discourse into courtroom, opera, or folklore.

Usual joke shapes:

- Announces a ridiculous principle with total warmth.
- Misunderstands data as a personal insult.
- Turns a minor incident into generational mythology.
- Walks into a sentence Split refuses to rescue.

Cartoon possibilities:

- His eye test becomes an actual giant eyeball with a referee kit.
- He summons pub logic as if it were legal precedent.
- He turns a fixture list into a treasure map.
- His old-school confidence physically inflates until Split taps it with one fact.

## Relationship Engine

Split and Peel work because they love each other and need each other.

- Split gives Peel structure, facts, and tactical guardrails.
- Peel gives Split warmth, chaos, old-school romance, and permission to be silly.
- Split is the lead voice; Peel is the accelerant.
- Peel is not dumb. He is emotionally intelligent, football-romantic, and often wrong in a useful way.
- Split is not cold. She is deeply invested, just faster at hiding it behind precision.

Default scene rhythm:

```text
Split frames the football truth.
Peel turns it into a feeling.
Split catches the exaggeration.
Peel doubles down with love.
The world becomes cartoon proof of their argument.
Split lands the clean button.
```

## Story Engines

Use these to generate repeatable premises.

### Fan Feeling Made Literal

A real supporter emotion becomes physically true in the studio: dread fog, confidence balloons, transfer-rumor gravity, derby static, VAR time loops.

### Tactics Vs Vibes

Split explains the match through shape, space, and decisions. Peel explains it through posture, aura, socks, and whether the player looks like he would win a duel in a car park.

### Football Bureaucracy Goes Mad

VAR, fixture congestion, transfer windows, injury reports, kit regulations, pundit panels, and fan polls behave like overpowered institutions.

### Tiny Detail Becomes A Saga

A throw-in, substitution, scarf, chant, graphic typo, warmup photo, or celebration becomes the entire episode's emotional conspiracy.

### The Club Is A Soap Opera

Managers, captains, substitutes, ultras, mascots, groundskeepers, social admins, and retired legends all behave like they are in a prestige drama, while the hosts try to keep it to sixty seconds.

### The Studio Cannot Contain The Match

The match leaks into the booth: grass grows over the desk, a keeper glove blocks the camera, a fan chant shakes the lower-third, stoppage time keeps extending the room.

## Running Bits And Seeds

Use sparingly and rotate them. Do not let any one bit become the default answer.

- "Put it in the match report."
- "The timeline has entered stoppage time."
- Split's forensic pause after Peel says something accidentally suggestive.
- Peel treating xG as a haunted spreadsheet.
- Split ranking meltdown stages like tactical phases.
- Peel's eye test becoming a literal object.
- Fanbases declaring eras after one match.
- The desk behaving like a stadium, courtroom, pub, or confessional.

## Guardrails

Do:

- Be specific about match facts, teams, and football culture.
- Keep the comedy affectionate toward fans.
- Let cartoons visualize true emotions.
- Build jokes from character conflict.
- Use callbacks only when they add clarity or pleasure.
- Make sure spoken lines use full team names and expanded weekdays.

Do not:

- Punch down.
- Use slurs or identity-based jokes.
- Harass real users, players, managers, or fans.
- Quote social posts verbatim unless explicitly cleared.
- Pretend a data source proves more than it proves.
- Let randomness replace story logic.
- Mock football itself as dumb.
- Reuse stale stock jokes when a fresh match-specific joke is available.

## Freshness Test

Before accepting an idea, ask:

- What real football detail makes this idea specific?
- Which character flaw causes the trouble?
- What emotion is being cartooned?
- How does the situation escalate?
- What does this reveal about Split, Peel, fans, or the game?
- Could the premise generate at least three visual gags?
- Does it leave the audience feeling the show loves football?
